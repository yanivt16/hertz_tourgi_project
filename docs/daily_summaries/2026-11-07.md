## 1. Starting point

Before today the repo held a from-scratch reimplementation of FUSE (Lee et al., 2026,
arXiv:2604.18547), a reproduction of the paper's 12 Best-of-N settings, and two label-free
extensions (verifier clustering, a majority-vote verifier). FUSE in the paper is **binary**
(is this candidate answer correct?) and works with **few verifiers (7–32) that give continuous
scores**, with missing scores imputed as 0.

Other work that landed on `main` today, from parallel sessions:

- The MLDS proposal style guide and the first version of our proposal (PR #1).
- Notes on the nine related papers, with full-text links (PRs #4, #5).

## 2. The DICES dataset

[DICES](https://github.com/google-research-datasets/dices-dataset) (Aroyo et al., 2023):
adversarial conversations with a chatbot (LaMDA), each rated by many people for whether the
final chatbot response is unsafe (Yes / Unsure / No).

| | DICES-350 | DICES-990 |
|---|---|---|
| Conversations | 350 | 990 |
| Raters | 123 | 172 |
| Ratings per conversation | 123 (everyone rated everything) | 69–76 (each rater rated a sample) |
| Rater × conversation matrix | complete | 58% empty |
| Expert safety label (`safety_gold`) | yes, 175 unsafe / 175 safe | **no** |
| Rater pool balanced by | gender × race/ethnicity | gender × locale (US, India) |

We published an explainer page on the dataset (structure, the full 123 × 350 rating matrix,
questionnaire, raters, topics, crowd vs. experts):
[claude.ai/artifact/771KB6KMA3VUGRKyS3WUHD](https://claude.ai/artifact/771KB6KMA3VUGRKyS3WUHD)
(private until shared).

**Mapping onto FUSE:** conversations are items, raters are verifiers, the expert label is the
hidden truth. There is no Best-of-N, so FUSE runs once on the whole matrix and is scored as a
binary classifier.

## 3. FUSE on DICES-350 (PR #2)

Accuracy against the expert label:

| Verifiers | Majority vote | Dawid–Skene | FUSE | FUSE − MV (95% CI) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 123 raters | 65.1 | 68.6 | 65.1 | +0.0 [−0.9, +0.9] |
| 10 race × gender groups | 65.1 | 68.0 | 70.9 (posterior) | +5.7 [+0.6, +10.9] |
| Raters' granular reason counts | 65.1 | 57.4 | 50.0 (broken) | −15.1 |

- **The crowd sets a ceiling.** Even with the threshold tuned on the expert labels, the share of
  raters saying "unsafe" reaches only 72.0%. Raters call 23–33% of conversations unsafe; the
  experts call 50% unsafe.
- **Per rater, FUSE is majority vote:** with 123 similar raters there is nothing to weight.
- **Few aggregated verifiers help FUSE:** 10 demographic groups give +5.7. With 30 finer groups
  the gain disappears, so this was one favourable configuration.
- **Granular reason counts break FUSE** (it estimates 1.5% unsafe and calls everything safe);
  the scale-free TCI statistic (`tci_standardize=True`) fixes it.

## 4. Do the methods make the same mistakes? (PR #3)

Yes. On the 123-rater view, majority vote, the naive mean and both FUSE predictors agree on
99–100% of conversations, and all label-free methods rank conversations almost identically
(AUC 78–79). They differ mainly in how many conversations they call unsafe.

- 89 conversations are wrong under all six label-free methods.
- **31 expert-unsafe conversations are missed by all nine methods**, including those that use
  labels. Only 11–26% of raters flag them; they cluster in gender/sexuality bias (10 of 22) and
  personal information (5 of 7).
- **No subset of raters sees them:** a rater's flag rate on them correlates 0.94 with their rate
  on expert-safe conversations, and every demographic group flags them less often than it flags
  expert-safe ones (17% vs 23%). Better aggregation of these ratings cannot recover them.

## 5. An LLM safety label for DICES (PR #7)

DICES-990 has no expert label, so we built a stand-in and validated it on DICES-350 first.

**Setup.**
- Provider: the plan started with Claude Sonnet 5.5 and switched to **OpenAI** at the user's request.
- Model: `gpt-6.1-terra`, chosen after comparing OpenAI's batch prices. It is newer and half the
  price of `gpt-5.6-terra`.
- Generation: reasoning effort `low`, strict JSON output (`rationale`, `unsafe`, 15 fixed categories).
- Evaluation split: DICES-350 split once into 174 dev / 176 test conversations, stratified by the
  expert label. The first 20 dev conversations were a pilot.

**Prompt development** (accuracy on the 154 dev conversations outside the pilot):

| Prompt | Idea | Accuracy | Called unsafe | Missed | False alarms |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| v1 | Neutral replies are safe | 64.9 | 22% | 49 | 5 |
| v2 | Only a clear rejection is safe | 61.7 | 60% | 22 | 37 |
| v3 | Drafted by a Claude Opus 5.5 subagent from v1, v2 and the 20 labelled pilot conversations | 68.2 | 51% | 24 | 25 |
| **v4** | v3 + fixes written from v3's dev errors | **74.0** | 42% | 27 | 13 |
| Crowd majority | | 63.6 | 25% | 48 | 8 |

- **Why v1 failed:** we read the model's input and rationales together. The prompt said neutral
  replies are safe, but for the experts a neutral reply to hateful content accepts it ("Trans
  people are gross" → "I'm sorry you feel that way" is unsafe). Their reason codes all start
  with `acceptance_`.
- **v2 overcorrected,** calling clean conversations unsafe.
- **v3:** a sentence-by-sentence check, with three named safe responses (disagree, explicitly
  decline, undercut the claim).
- **v4 added:**
  - doubt, or a question back, is safe;
  - moving to another topic is safe;
  - general advice on the user's own medical, legal or relationship problem is unsafe;
  - enthusiasm for regulated goods is unsafe;
  - risky suggestions involving children are unsafe;
  - agreeing with a political slogan takes a side.
- **The rule to pick v4 was fixed in advance:** v4 if it beats v3 on the 154 non-pilot dev
  conversations. v4 was written before the test results were read.

**Held-out test (176 conversations no prompt was written from):**

| Labeller | Accuracy | Kappa | Called unsafe | vs crowd majority (95% CI) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| **v4 (final)** | **69.9** | 0.40 | 38% | +4.5 [−2.8, +11.9] |
| v3 | 67.0 | 0.34 | 55% | +1.7 [−8.5, +11.4] |
| Crowd majority | 65.3 | 0.31 | 21% | |

- **Agreement with the single expert verdict stays near 70%** for every labeller, crowd or LLM.
  Part of that is the expert label itself: near-identical responses get opposite expert labels.
- **The LLM and the crowd are right on different conversations.** On test (v4): LLM only on 26,
  crowd only on 18, both on 97, neither on 35.

**DICES-990 labels:**
- Coverage: 990 of 990 labelled, no refusals or errors.
- Result: the LLM calls 40.8% unsafe, the crowd majority 15.7%; the two agree on 71.4%.

**Operational problems and fixes:**
- **Stuck batches.** OpenAI batches stalled on their last few requests. The script now cancels
  after a time limit, keeps the finished results and sends the rest directly.
- **Queue limit.** The organization's queue limit (1.35M tokens per model, which also counts each
  request's maximum output) rejected the 990 batch twice. We added a parallel direct mode and
  labelled the 990 that way.

**Cost:** under about $4.10 in total at list prices, at most $2.87 of it for the 990. The
metadata prices cached input at the full rate, so the real figure is lower.

## 6. FUSE on DICES-990 (PR #7)

Agreement with the LLM label, on all 990 conversations:

| Verifiers | Missing | Majority vote | Dawid–Skene | FUSE logistic | FUSE − MV (95% CI) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 172 raters, missing filled with 0 | 58% | 71.4 | **77.5** | 71.0 | −0.4 [−2.1, +1.4] |
| 157 raters with ≥ 50 ratings | 54% | 71.4 | 77.6 | 71.5 | +0.1 [−1.7, +1.9] |
| 4 locale × gender groups | 0% | 71.7 | 74.3 | 73.0 | +1.3 [−2.5, +5.1] |
| 6 locale × age groups | 0% | 71.4 | 74.8 | 74.5 | +3.1 [−0.5, +6.9] |
| 7 locale × race groups | 0% | 71.4 | 74.0 | **77.5** | **+6.1 [+2.9, +9.2]** |

- **On sparse individual raters FUSE is majority vote.** Filling the gaps with zeros (as the paper
  does) distorts its correlations: its ranking (AUC 79.8) is even slightly worse than majority
  vote's (82.2). Dawid–Skene, which skips missing ratings, gains 6 points.
- **On demographic groups (no missing entries) FUSE beats majority vote,** most on locale × race.
- **Calibration on DICES-350:** the same runs scored against the experts and against the LLM
  label order the methods the same way. The LLM label shifts the level (methods agree more with
  it), not the ranking.

## 7. Block by block: the missing-data fix (PR #8)

**Idea (from the user):**
- Split the 990 conversations into groups that share as many raters as possible, each with at
  least 100 conversations.
- Run every group separately.

**What we found in the data:**
- DICES-990 was collected in batches: 28 raters rated about 90% or more of all conversations,
  and conversations cluster by rater overlap.
- Average-linkage clustering of the rater sets (Jaccard) gives **5 blocks of 159–330
  conversations**. This is the largest split where every block keeps at least 100.
- In each block, **41–56 core raters** rated at least 90% of it, so the block is under 1% empty
  instead of 58%.

| Pooled over 990, agreement with the LLM label | Whole sparse matrix | Blocks, core raters | Blocks: − majority vote (95% CI) |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Majority vote | 71.4 | 70.5 | |
| Dawid–Skene | 77.5 | 76.3 | +5.8 [+2.8, +8.7] |
| FUSE logistic | 71.0 | 74.4 | +3.9 [+2.1, +5.8] |
| FUSE posterior | 70.9 | **75.6** | **+5.1 [+3.1, +7.1]** |

- **Against majority vote:** FUSE wins in all 5 blocks (+1.3 to +6.7; significant in 3).
- **Against Dawid–Skene:** level. FUSE posterior − Dawid–Skene is −0.7 [−3.0, +1.5].
- **Number of blocks:** 3 or 4 blocks give +5.3 and +4.9, so the choice of 5 doesn't matter.
- **Density is what helps, not the split:** the same blocks with all their raters (21–37% empty
  inside the block) give only +0.5 to +1.8.

## 8. Why FUSE trails Dawid–Skene on crowd data

Dawid–Skene (1979) is the classic crowdsourcing model:
- Each annotator has a sensitivity and a specificity, learned jointly with the hidden labels by EM.
- It uses only the votes each annotator actually gave.
- It works natively on discrete votes.
- Its maximum-likelihood fit uses all the data.

FUSE estimates annotator quality from second- and third-order moments, needs a complete matrix,
and was designed for continuous scores.

- **In the FUSE paper,** Dawid–Skene is one of the extra baselines (App. E.8, Table 9), and those
  baselines "uniformly fall below FUSE". In our reproduction FUSE beats it in all 12 settings,
  by 2–11 points. There the verifiers are few, continuous and complete, and Dawid–Skene must
  binarize their scores.
- **On sparse crowd data the situation reverses.** Dawid–Skene's gain is also largely the cut
  point: it calls about 38% unsafe where the crowd majority calls 16%.
- **Lesson:** on human annotators the real baseline to beat is Dawid–Skene, not majority vote,
  and missing data is FUSE's main weakness there.

## 9. Proposal and scope

- **Two versions in `docs/fuse_crowd_proposal/`:** the original and the Reichman-format one
  (PR #6). Mentor: Dr. Regev Cohen.
- **Stage 1:** does FUSE beat majority vote (and Dawid–Skene) on human labels with a gold label?
- **Stage 2:** does a model trained on FUSE labels beat one trained on a single annotator?
- **We confirmed FUSE is binary-only in the original paper.** The hidden label is ±1; the input
  scores are continuous and FUSE binarizes them.

**Scope decision (today):**
- **Focus on binary tasks.** Multi-class (one-vs-rest) moves to an optional extension.
- **Stage 2 uses a pretrained text encoder** instead of a CNN on images.
- **Reason:** the core question can be answered fully on binary data, and the gap DICES exposed
  is missing data, not the number of classes.
- **Not yet in the proposal files.** They still mention multi-class, CIFAR-10H and LabelMe, so
  the advisor message presents the narrowing as a proposed change.

Candidate binary datasets with gold for Stage 1:

| Dataset | Gold | Annotators per item | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| DICES-350 | experts (350) | 123 | done |
| D_PosSent (Zheng et al., 2017) | all 1,000 tweets | 20 of 85 (~24% filled) | MV 93.31 vs D&S 96.00 in the paper; data must be located |
| RTE (Snow et al., 2008) | yes | ~10 | check that annotator ids are released |
| TEMP (Snow et al., 2008) | yes | ~10 | same source as RTE |

## 10. Key findings

1. **On many interchangeable raters FUSE reduces to majority vote** (DICES-350 and DICES-990).
2. **FUSE helps with a few aggregated verifiers:** demographic groups, +5.7 on DICES-350 and
   +6.1 on DICES-990.
3. **Missing data is FUSE's main weakness on crowd data.** Zero-imputation costs it about 6 points
   against Dawid–Skene. On dense blocks of the same data it beats majority vote by 5.1 and ties
   Dawid–Skene.
4. **The crowd itself caps accuracy against experts (about 72%).** A set of expert-unsafe
   conversations (mostly gender bias and personal information) is invisible to every rater
   subgroup.
5. **An LLM label can stand in where there is no gold,** at about 70% agreement with experts, and
   it errs on different conversations than the crowd.
6. **On human annotators the baseline to beat is Dawid–Skene.**

## 11. Open issues and next steps

**Open issues:**
- FUSE needs missing-data handling without imputation: moments computed only over items both
  annotators rated (pairwise-complete), as the proposal plans. The block trick works only when
  data was collected in batches.
- The DICES expert label is one per conversation and not always consistent; the LLM label is
  only an approximation.
- FUSE assumes conditional independence; human raters share a common factor (general leniency).

**Next steps:**
1. Stage 1 on 2–3 binary datasets with gold: DICES-350, D_PosSent, RTE.
2. In parallel, missing-data handling in FUSE. The candidate datasets are sparser than DICES-990,
   so without it we would mostly be measuring the zero-imputation.
3. Stage 2: a text model trained on FUSE labels vs. a single annotator's labels.
4. Later, as extensions: an LLM as an extra verifier, and multi-class.
5. Update both proposal files to the binary scope if the advisor agrees.

**Questions for the advisor:**
1. Is DICES a good fit for Stage 1?
2. Is an LLM label a legitimate reference where there's no gold?
3. Should Dawid–Skene become the main baseline in the midterm criterion?
4. Does he agree with the binary focus?
